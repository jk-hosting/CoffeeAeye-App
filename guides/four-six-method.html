Brewing guide

The 4:6 method, explained.

Tetsu Kasuya won the 2016 World Brewers Cup with a V60 recipe that does something no other recipe does: it separates the two things you actually want to control, and gives you a dial for each.

The idea in one paragraph

Divide your brew water into two parts — the first 40% and the remaining 60%. The first 40% sets the balance between sweetness and acidity. The last 60% sets the strength. Change one without touching the other. That is the whole method, and it is why it survives as a teaching recipe long after most competition routines are forgotten.

The standard recipe

20 g coffee, 300 g water (1:15) · coarse grind, coarser than you would normally use on a V60 · water at 92–93 °C · five pours, one every 45 seconds · finished around 3:30.

The pours

TimePourRunning total
0:0060 g60 g
0:4560 g120 g  — end of the 40%
1:3060 g180 g
2:1560 g240 g
3:0060 g300 g

Let the bed draw down between pours. If it hasn't cleared by the time the next pour is due, your grind is too fine for this recipe.

Dial one: sweetness against acidity

The first two pours make up the 40%. Their total stays at 120 g — what you change is how it is split between them.

SplitResult
50 g then 70 gSweeter, rounder, lower perceived acidity
60 g then 60 gBalanced — start here
70 g then 50 gBrighter, more acidity, more clarity

The rule to remember: a smaller first pour gives you more sweetness, a larger first pour gives you more acidity. On a washed Ethiopian that is already screamingly bright, go smaller. On a sleepy washed Brazil that tastes flat, go larger.

Dial two: strength

The remaining 180 g is where you set concentration, and again the total does not change — only the number of pours it is broken into.

PoursResult
1 × 180 gLightest, most tea-like
2 × 90 gMedium body
3 × 60 gStrongest, fullest body — Kasuya's own

More pours means more agitation and more contact with fresh water, so more extraction. Fewer pours means a gentler, lighter cup.

Why the grind is coarse

Five separate pours add up to a long total brew time, and a normal V60 grind would over-extract badly by the end. Going coarser buys back the time. If your brew is finishing well past 3:30, or the bed is stalling between pours, coarsen further before you change anything else.

Start a step or two coarser than the V60 row on your grinder's chart — on a Comandante C40 that means moving up from around 18 clicks, on a 1Zpresso JX-Pro up from around 46.

Common ways it goes wrong

  • Grind too fine. The single most common fault. Long draw-downs, harsh finish, brew running to 4:30 or beyond.
  • Pouring too fast. Each 60 g should take roughly 10 seconds. Dumping it in floods the bed and destroys the control the method is built on.
  • Chasing both dials at once. Change the 40% split or the number of 60% pours — not both. The entire point is that they are independent.
  • Scaling badly. Keep 1:15 and keep the percentages. For 15 g use 225 g water, so the 40% is 90 g and the 60% is 135 g. The 4:6 calculator does this for any dose and prints every pour weight and time.
