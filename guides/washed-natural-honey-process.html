Brewing guide

Washed, natural and honey.

A coffee seed arrives inside a fruit. How that fruit is removed — before drying, after drying, or partway — changes the cup more than the country it grew in. It is the most useful word on the bag.

The three, side by side

ProcessWhat happensHow it tastes
WashedFruit stripped, seed fermented and rinsed, then driedClean, bright, acidity-forward, lighter bodied
NaturalWhole cherry dried intact, fruit removed afterwardsHeavy, sweet, fruity, sometimes fermented or boozy
HoneySkin removed, sticky mucilage left on to dryBetween the two — sweet with more body than washed, cleaner than natural

Washed

The fruit comes off first. The seed ferments in water for a day or so to break down the remaining mucilage, gets rinsed, and dries clean. Nothing from the fruit is left to influence the seed during drying.

The result is transparency. What you taste is the bean itself — the varietal, the altitude, the terroir — with acidity that arrives clean and defined rather than wrapped in sweetness. This is why washed coffees are the reference point for judging origin character, and why a washed Ethiopian tastes like citrus and florals while a natural from the same farm tastes like blueberry jam.

It is also the least forgiving. There is nothing to hide behind, so under-extraction shows up immediately as sourness.

Natural

The whole cherry dries with the seed inside, over weeks on raised beds. Sugars and fruit compounds migrate inward, and a slow fermentation happens inside the drying fruit.

The result is weight and sweetness: berry, tropical fruit, heavier body, lower perceived acidity. At its best it is extraordinary. Done carelessly it goes over-fermented and tastes of solvent or rotting fruit — naturals carry more risk than washed coffees because the drying is harder to control.

Brewing-wise, naturals are more soluble than washed coffees at the same roast level. They give up their solubles faster, so start a touch coarser than you would for a washed coffee and expect a shorter drawdown.

Honey

The skin is removed but the sticky mucilage — the "honey", nothing to do with actual honey — is left on to dry. How much stays on determines the style, usually colour-coded:

  • White / yellow honey — most mucilage removed, dries fast. Closest to washed.
  • Red honey — more left on, slower drying. Noticeably sweeter and rounder.
  • Black honey — nearly all left on, longest drying. Closest to a natural, heavy and syrupy.

Honey processing is the deliberate middle: the body and sweetness of a natural with more of the clarity of a washed coffee. Costa Rica built a reputation on it.

Anaerobic and experimental

Fermentation in a sealed tank without oxygen, sometimes with added yeast or fruit, for a controlled length of time. The flavours produced are intense, unusual and genuinely divisive — cinnamon, rum, tropical fruit, and at the far end something closer to a sour beer than to coffee.

Two practical notes. These coffees frequently need a coarser grind than their roast level suggests, because the fermentation has already broken down structure inside the bean and they extract quickly. And the label rarely tells you how far the producer pushed it, so treat the first brew from an anaerobic bag as a genuine unknown.

These are starting offsets from your usual setting, not absolute numbers — your grinder's specific figures are on its grind chart. Change one thing per brew and let taste move you, using the dial-in method.

Process beats country

If you take one thing from this: when you are guessing how a new bag will behave, the process word tells you more than the origin does. A natural Ethiopian and a natural Brazilian have more in common with each other, in how they extract, than a natural Ethiopian has with a washed one from the next valley.
