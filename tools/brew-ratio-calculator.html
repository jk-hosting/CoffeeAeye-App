Grams, not millilitres

Water is weighed, not measured by volume. One millilitre of water weighs one gram closely enough that the two are interchangeable in a brewing context, so a scale replaces a jug and removes an entire class of error. If a recipe gives you millilitres, weigh that number of grams and move on.

Ratio is strength. Grind is extraction.

This is the distinction that makes the calculator useful rather than decorative. Ratio controls how much coffee ends up dissolved in the cup — how strong it tastes. Grind controls how much of the available material comes out of the grounds — whether it tastes sour, balanced or bitter.

So if a brew is sour or harsh, the ratio is not your problem; go to the dial-in guide and move the grind. If the flavours are clean and the cup is simply too weak or too intense, leave the grind alone and change the ratio here. Confusing the two is the single most common reason people chase the same coffee in circles.

Brew ratio and what actually reaches the cup

A V60 does not give you back all the water you pour. The bed and the paper hold roughly two grams for every gram of dry coffee, so 15 g of coffee and 250 g of water yields around 220 g of drink rather than 250 g. That is normal and it is already accounted for in the ratio bands above. It matters only if you are measuring beverage weight for an extraction yield figure, where the number you need is what came out, not what went in.
